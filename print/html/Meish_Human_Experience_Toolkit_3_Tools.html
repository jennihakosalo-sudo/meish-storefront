Quick start

Three short tools for looking at an experience honestly. Print them, take a pen, and go to the actual place — a room, a service, a website, a meeting, a morning. Write in ordinary words. Description first, judgement later.

01NoticeMeish Sensory Walk

Describe what is actually here through the senses, before anyone proposes a change. Two pages, one walk.

02ShapeMeish Atmosphere Builder

Decide the feeling you want, then turn it into light, sound, materials, spatial cues and human behaviour.

03Remove frictionMeish Points of Confusion Presence

Collect the moments where a person had to guess, search, wait or check, and work out what they needed instead.

Each tool works on its own. Used in sequence they answer three different questions: what is here, what we want, and what is in the way.

Working alone is quicker and often more honest. A pair sees more. A small team of three to five brings disagreement, which is useful — fill the sheets separately first, then compare.

Solo
30–45 min
Pair
45–60 min
Small team
60–90 min
  • A printed copy for each person, and a pen
  • The real place, not a photograph or a memory of it
  • Permission to be slow for half an hour
  • Somewhere to put the finished sheets where they will be read again

Nothing here has to be filled in completely. A half-finished sheet with three honest observations is worth more than a tidy one written from memory afterwards.

Rule one

Describe before you explain. Write what is there before writing what it means.

Rule two

Do not solve anything until the closing section of each tool.

Meish working tool — 01

Meish Sensory Walk

Notice what a place, service, product or moment is actually doing before trying to improve it.

Walk through it at ordinary speed and fix nothing yet. Describe what is there in plain words, as if telling someone who cannot see it. Opinions can wait until the last section.

Time of day

01

What is here

Visual environment

What do you see first, second, third? Describe the light, the surfaces, and what is competing for attention.

Sound

What can you hear once you stop talking? Name the constant sounds and the sudden ones.

Touch and material

What do you actually touch here, and how does it feel — warm, cold, smooth, sticky, heavy, flimsy?

Scent

What does the air smell of? If it smells of nothing, say whether that reads as clean or as empty.

What it does to a body

Temperature

Are you too warm, too cold, or unaware of it? Where does that change as you move through?

Movement

Where do people go, pause, hesitate or turn back? Describe the route your body wants to take.

Bodily response

What does your body do here? Note your shoulders, your breathing, your pace, your hands, your eyes.

Emotional tone

Meish working tool — 02

Meish Atmosphere Builder

Choose what a moment should feel like, and turn that intended feeling into concrete sensory and behavioural signals.

An atmosphere is not decoration. It is the sum of small decisions people can feel but rarely name. Decide the feeling first, then decide what would carry it.

Who it is for
Date

02

Signals that carry it

Write what each one would actually look like here — the thing, not the principle.

Light

How bright, from where, what is left in shadow?

Sound

What is heard, what is quieter, what never here?

Materials

What do people touch, sit on, carry, put down?

Scent

What should the air do? Restraint is usually right.

First move

Choose one change, not a programme. Atmosphere shifts through small repeated decisions rather than one large gesture.

Change this first

The smallest change with the largest felt difference.

Find where a person must guess, search, wait, remember, compare or check — and remove the mental load that does not need to be there.
